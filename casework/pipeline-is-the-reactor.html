Casework · Diagnostic

The pipeline is the reactor.

A chloramine residual investigation on a regional bulk water transfer main · Peter Hillis, in a previous role

A chloramine residual investigation on a regional bulk water transfer main. Delivered by our principal in a previous role; described here at the level of the question, the method and what it demonstrated. Client, site and pipeline are not identified and figures are rounded.

The question

A bulk water transfer main carries treated, chloraminated water from a regional source system to a receiving treatment plant tens of kilometres away. Partway along, a boosting facility adds sodium hypochlorite and liquid ammonium sulphate through injection quills into a dosing spool, passes the flow through a static mixer, and sends it on. The receiving plant breakpoints the arriving chloramine to free chlorine and re-chloraminates for distribution.

The residual arriving at the receiving plant was consistently lower than the boosting facility’s dose should have delivered. The working hypothesis when the assessment was commissioned was that the two chemicals were not mixing well enough at the injection point, and the scope was written around that hypothesis: benchmark the static mixer against established mixing criteria and against two other chloramination installations, and consider whether preformed monochloramine or a different ammonia reagent would do better.

The engagement answered a different question, because the evidence pointed somewhere else.

Treating the system as a reactor

Each of those has to be known before any one of them can be blamed.

The method was to stop treating the injection point as the problem and treat the whole system, from the point of injection to the point of measurement, as a chemical reactor with a residence time, a set of reaction pathways, an inlet composition and an instrument at the outlet. Each of those has to be known before any one of them can be blamed.

Residence time. The assessment had inherited a residence time of about 24 hours. Reconstructing it from the flow records rather than from the nominal design flow gave a figure closer to 40 hours. The main was operated intermittently, with the boosting facility running for part of each day and the water standing in the pipe between duty periods. Water spending substantially longer in the system than the design basis assumed, including periods of zero flow, has far more opportunity for residual decay before any question of mixing arises.

Reaction pathways. For this investigation, monochloramine loss was considered through three principal pathways: bulk decay in the water itself, pipe-wall demand, and biological nitrification where free ammonia and time permitted it. These have different dependencies on temperature, ratio, surface area and flow, and they respond to different operational levers, so they have to be separated. A dynamic two-pathway model was built for the bulk and wall routes, calibrated against a matched pair of field measurements at the two ends of the main, with the intermittent duty represented explicitly. Under the calibrated conditions roughly two-thirds of the loss was bulk decay and one-third wall decay. Nitrification was assessed within the same framework using the nitrite record and the free-ammonia stoichiometry, and could not be excluded.

Inlet composition. The water arriving at the boosting facility already carried a chloramine residual from the source system. The boost therefore had to be understood as an addition to an existing chlorine-to-nitrogen ratio, not as formation from zero, and the target ratio had to be set against the combined chemistry rather than the dose alone. Checking the chemical mass balance at the facility surfaced something the residual data alone would never have shown: the delivered strength of the dosing chemicals was inconsistent with what the control system assumed. A dose that was correct in the control logic was not the dose entering the pipe.

Control architecture. The dosing arrangement and its control philosophy were reviewed as a system: how flow pacing, ratio setpoints and any residual feedback combined to decide what was injected, what each element assumed about the others, and where an error in one would propagate silently. Two things mattered. First, the control logic was doing arithmetic on reagent strengths that had not been verified, so its ratio control was only as good as an assumed number. Second, the feedback available to the control system came from an analyser whose own consistency had not been established. A controller that trusts an unverified strength on the way in and an unverified measurement on the way out is not controlling the ratio; it is controlling a belief about the ratio. The review concluded that reagent strength verification and analyser reconfiguration had to precede any change to setpoints, because tuning the control system against an inconsistent measurement is how a second problem gets created.

Sampling and measurement. The online analyser’s chlorine and ammonia channels were checked against each other on nitrogen stoichiometry and found not to be internally consistent: the total chlorine, monochloramine and free ammonia readings did not close the way the chemistry requires. The measurement system was therefore part of the uncertainty, not an independent reference against which to diagnose the process. The field data were then re-read with that in mind. Where the analysis needed a value it could rely on, it used matched grab samples taken at both ends of the main within the same water parcel, allowing for the transit time, rather than the continuous record, and the decay model was calibrated to that matched pair. Sampling location and timing were treated as part of the reactor: a sample drawn during a stagnant period, or from a point that sees a different residence time from the bulk flow, describes a different reactor from the one being modelled.

A controller that trusts an unverified strength on the way in and an unverified measurement on the way out is not controlling the ratio. It is controlling a belief about the ratio.

Mixing. The original hypothesis was not discarded; it was tested. The static mixer was checked against the operating velocity and the mixing calculations showed it should achieve adequate homogeneity. Mixing was retained in the assessment as a screened, lower-priority factor rather than the principal mechanism, and the detailed CFD comparison in the original scope was not pursued, because it would not have changed the diagnosis or the recommended actions. That decision was recorded, with its reasoning, in the report’s scope compliance matrix.

What the evidence showed

Put together, the evidence did not support mixing as the principal cause of the residual loss. What it did support was a longer, intermittently operated reactor than the design basis assumed, with decay chemistry that had never been quantified for this water, feeding an instrument that was not measuring quite what everyone thought it was measuring, from a dosing system whose reagent strength had drifted from its assumed value.

Each of those is individually ordinary. None of them was visible from the residual trend, and the trend had been read as a mixing problem for long enough that a mixing study had been commissioned to fix it.

What changed as a result

Three operational levers were quantified: dose, chlorine-to-nitrogen ratio, and plant duty. Their relative effect on the delivered residual was ranked so the operator could see which adjustment bought the most residual for the least cost and risk. Chemical strength verification and analyser reconfiguration were identified as prerequisites for any further adjustment, because tuning a dose against an inconsistent measurement is how a second problem gets created.

The investigation also stated what it did not know, and what it would take to know it. The calibrated model rested on one matched field measurement, a wall decay coefficient transferred from published work on comparable pipe, an unmeasured water temperature, and an ammonia response that had not been measured at the site. Those became the next phase: temperature instrumentation on the main, bench-scale decay studies on the actual water, a pipe-section reactor to measure wall decay directly, extraction of the true duty cycle from SCADA, formation-potential testing, and a biological baseline so that nitrification could be confirmed or excluded rather than inferred.

Evidence classes in this work

The report carried an evidence class against every material claim, and the pattern is instructive because it is typical of operational investigations:

Three of the load-bearing findings rest on transferred or assumed values. Saying so explicitly is what allowed the next phase to be scoped around the evidence that would actually settle the question rather than around the original hypothesis.

What it demonstrates

That is the difference between delivering the scope and answering the question.

Frame before measuring. The original scope framed the problem as mixing. Re-framing it as a reactor with a residence time, pathways, an inlet and an instrument made the mixing hypothesis one of several, and testable.

Check the instrument and the controller before the chemistry. Two of the most consequential findings were about measurement and reagent strength, not reaction kinetics, and the control architecture was quietly relying on both. Neither would have been found by a better mixer.

Reconstruct, don’t inherit. The residence time had been carried from an earlier document. Rebuilding it from the operating record changed the diagnosis on its own.

Say what the model rests on. A calibrated model is only as strong as its calibration data and its transferred coefficients. Naming them turned the report’s uncertainty into a costed investigation programme rather than a caveat.

Reweight the scope on evidence. The engagement spent its effort where the evidence pointed and recorded why the original benchmarking exercise was not pursued. That is the difference between delivering the scope and answering the question.
