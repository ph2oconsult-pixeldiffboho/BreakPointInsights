Casework · Capacity, Evidence

Quantified risk. Defensible investment.

A supply-demand and service-risk model for a pretreatment capacity decision · Peter Hillis, in a previous role

A daily supply-demand model linking raw-water quality, plant production, demand and storage, used to size and stage a pretreatment capacity upgrade against an agreed service-risk target. Client and site are not identified; the figures below are from the June 2026 planning example and are rounded.

A water utility was planning a pretreatment upgrade sized, as most are, against a combination of peak demand, adverse raw-water quality and conservative operating assumptions layered on top of one another. Conventional studies treat these as though they occur together. They rarely do, and treating them as though they do produces one of two failures: overstating the capacity required, because the worst case in every input is not the worst case for the system as a whole; or, just as often, understating the genuine service risk, because a scenario that matters gets buried inside an average.

Planning under uncertainty

Without probability, duration and timing attached to the inputs, a capacity study cannot distinguish a rare, brief, low-consequence event from a frequent, sustained one that actually threatens supply. Both can produce the same headline number in a conventional study. Only one of them should drive a capital decision.

An integrated supply-risk model

The alternative is to combine daily operating records with future demand and raw-water scenarios, and carry probability, duration and timing through every stage rather than collapsing it into a single design point.

Raw-water qualityFrequency, duration and timing of adverse conditions, not just their severity.
Plant productionCapacity as a function of the water quality actually being treated, not a single rated figure.
Seasonal demandThe daily demand profile and its growth, not just the annual peak.
Storage responseHow storage draws down and recovers under each scenario, day by day.

Carried through together, these produce a service-risk output: the probability, duration and severity of a supply shortfall, for any given capacity. An agreed service-risk target then gives a defensible basis for sizing and staging investment, in place of a single deterministic design case.

Capacity against risk

The output is a curve, not a number: the annual probability of a conserve-water notice, plotted against pretreatment capacity as a proportion of the proposed upgrade. In the June 2026 example, the utility had adopted a target of no more than a 10 per cent annual chance of a conserve-water notice. Fifty per cent of the proposed pretreatment capacity was the smallest tested option that met it.

21.2% → 7.6%Annual conserve-water notice risk, before and after the selected option.
50%Of the proposed pretreatment capacity: the smallest tested option below the target.

What it supported

The model did not produce a single answer so much as a basis for three decisions, in order: agree the service-risk target before selecting a capacity, so the target is a policy choice made deliberately rather than an artefact of whichever scenario the study happened to run; compare treatment, storage and demand-response options on the same probability basis, so a capital solution is not compared against an operational one on different terms; and stage the upgrade against demand and raw-water risk as they actually develop, rather than build once against a single future.

What it demonstrates

The same discipline runs through this case as through every BreakPoint engagement. The decision was framed as a risk target, not a design flow. The evidence was daily operating records, not an annual average. The reasoning combined four linked models rather than one. The result was challenged against the alternative of just building to the peak case, and found to cost more for less defensible risk reduction. And the decision was stated with its evidence attached: a curve a board can read, not a number they have to trust.
