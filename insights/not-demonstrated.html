Insights · Evidence integrity

Not demonstrated is not the same as adequate.

Peter Hillis, Founder and Principal · September 2026

Every major water infrastructure decision rests on a stack of statements. Some are measured. Some are calculated from measurements. Some are borrowed from the literature, some come out of a model, and some are simply assumed because the project needed a number and nobody had a better one. By the time the stack reaches a board paper, the labels have usually fallen off. What remains is a set of confident sentences, and the reader has no way to tell which of them would survive being asked "how do you know?"

This piece is about one specific failure in that stack, because it is the one I see most often and the one that does the most damage. It is the quiet conversion of "we have no evidence this is a problem" into "this is not a problem."

How the conversion happens

It rarely happens in one step. It happens over a sequence of documents, each of which is individually reasonable.

A concept study notes that a clarifier’s solids loading under flood conditions has not been assessed, and records it as a gap to be closed in the next phase. The next phase is under time pressure, so the gap is carried forward with a line saying "no issues identified." The detailed design report summarises the earlier phases and writes "clarifier performance under design conditions confirmed." The business case quotes the design report. The board sees a plant whose clarifiers have been confirmed.

Nobody lied. Three documents later, an absence of assessment has become a positive finding.

Nobody lied. Each author summarised the document before them in good faith, and each summary dropped a caveat. Three documents later, an absence of assessment has become a positive finding. I think of it as evidence laundering, and like the financial kind it works precisely because every individual transaction looks clean.

The same thing happens with vendor claims. A supplier states that a membrane achieves a given log removal. The claim is supported by a challenge test at another site, on different water, at a different flux. The design report records the log removal as a design basis. The regulator asks for the evidence and is given the supplier’s brochure. Somewhere in that sequence the claim stopped being "reported by the supplier under different conditions" and became "demonstrated."

Why it matters more than it looks

The cost of the conversion is not that a wrong number gets used. Often the number is fine. The cost is that the decision loses its sensitivity map. Nobody can any longer say which of the hundred statements in the design basis are load-bearing, and of those, which are actually supported. Once the provenance is lost, a project can no longer distinguish the assumptions that barely matter from the ones capable of changing the decision.

When something goes wrong at commissioning, or five years into operation, the first question is always the same: what did we assume? And the answer is usually that the assumption was recorded somewhere, three documents back, with a caveat that was never carried forward. The project did not lack the information. It lacked the discipline to keep the information attached to the number.

I have sat in more than one review where an experienced engineer, asked how confident the team was in a capacity figure, said "very confident," and when asked what that confidence rested on, could produce only the fact that the figure had appeared in every document for two years. Repetition had become evidence.

The remedy is boring, and that is the point

The fix is not a new method. It is a labelling discipline applied consistently enough that the labels survive summarisation.

At BreakPoint every decision-relevant claim, conclusion and assumption in a technical output carries one of seven evidence classes:

  • Measured. Directly observed on the asset or in the laboratory, with provenance.
  • Calculated. Derived from measured values by a stated method.
  • Literature-supported. Established in authoritative sources, with the transfer conditions checked.
  • Modelled. The output of a stated model with stated inputs and stated limitations.
  • Inferred. Reasoned from indirect evidence, with the reasoning shown.
  • Assumed. Adopted without direct evidence, flagged with its severity and the evidence that would resolve it.
  • Heuristic. A rule of thumb, used only where its bounds are declared.

A statement cannot move up the list without new evidence.

The classes are ordinary. What makes them work is the rule that a statement cannot move up the list without new evidence. "Assumed" does not become "Calculated" because someone ran a spreadsheet on it. "Reported by the supplier" does not become "Demonstrated" because it appeared in three reports. If the class of a statement has not changed, its label does not change, however many times it is quoted.

Applied consistently, this does two things. It makes the laundering visible, because a summary that drops a caveat now has to change a label, and changing a label is a decision someone has to own. And it produces, almost for free, the sensitivity map that the decision needs: sort the design basis by evidence class and by consequence, and the assumptions that are both load-bearing and unsupported are at the top of the list.

Two questions that break the spell

If you are reading a report rather than writing one, two questions do most of the work.

The first is "what would we expect to see if this were wrong?" A statement that has actually been demonstrated has a falsifier. A clarifier that has been assessed under flood loading has a solids flux calculation, a settling test, or operating data at high loads, and if the clarifier were undersized one of those would show it. A statement with no falsifier has not been demonstrated, whatever the document says.

The second is "where did this number first appear, and what did it say then?" Trace it back. It is remarkable how often the origin is a design guide value, a supplier figure, or a placeholder from a concept study, and how often the original author was careful to say so.

A note on the word "conservative"

“Conservative” without a stated direction is another way of saying “not examined.”

One more conversion deserves a mention. When an assumption is challenged, the most common defence is that it is conservative. Sometimes it is. But conservative in which direction, and for which failure mode? A high assumed influent load is conservative for tank sizing and unconservative for the energy and chemical estimate. A low assumed peak factor is conservative for the operating cost and unconservative for the hydraulic capacity. "Conservative" without a stated direction is another way of saying "not examined."

What this looks like in practice

None of this requires a new tool, though we have built some. It requires that the person responsible for a technical recommendation can, for each statement it depends on, say how it is known and what would change it. That is the standard we hold our own work to, including the marketing.

Absence of evidence is a finding. It is a finding that something has not been examined. Treating it as a finding that something is adequate is a remarkably reliable way to turn a well-run project into an expensive investigation.
