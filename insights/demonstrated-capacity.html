What “demonstrated capacity” actually means.

Peter Hillis, Founder and Principal · September 2026

Ask a plant what its capacity is and you will get a number. Ask five people at the utility the same question and you may get five different numbers, each of them correct. The one on the nameplate. The one in the licence. The one the process engineer calculated. The one operations will actually run to. The one the board was told when the last upgrade was approved.

They differ because "capacity" is doing the work of at least five distinct concepts, and most capacity studies never separate them. This piece sets out the five, explains why the gaps between them are where the money is, and describes how we go about determining the one that matters.

Five numbers that get confused

Nominal capacity is the design flow. It is the figure on the drawings and in the licence, and it usually came from a design guide loading rate applied to a tank volume or a filter area at the time the plant was built. It says something about what the designer intended. It says nothing about what the plant does.

Theoretical capacity is what the process calculations say each unit could handle under stated conditions. It is calculated, usually competently, and usually for one influent condition. Its limitation is that it is built from individual unit calculations, each of which assumes the units upstream and downstream are behaving. Real plants are coupled systems. A clarifier that can theoretically take the flow may not be able to take it while the filters are backwashing and the recycle is running.

Demonstrated capacity is what the plant has actually achieved, sustained, while meeting its obligations. It is measured. It is also, in my experience, almost never written down. The operating record contains it, but somebody has to go and find the periods where flow was high, water quality was hard, and the plant still met its targets, and then ask what was happening at every stage during those periods. That is a different job from reading the SCADA maximum.

Constrained capacity is the flow at which the first binding constraint bites. The binding constraint is rarely the unit everyone worries about. It is the sludge handling system that cannot keep up with the solids produced under flood turbidity. It is the chemical dosing pump that runs out of turndown at low flow, not high. It is the disinfection contact time that falls below the required Ct at peak flow with the reservoir drawn down. It is the operator who cannot physically manage the number of backwashes per shift. Constrained capacity is where the first real-world limitation becomes binding. It will often be lower than the theoretical figure, because the theoretical calculation does not capture every coupled constraint.

Resilient capacity is the flow the plant can sustain when something has gone wrong: a unit out of service, a raw water event, an equipment failure, a staff shortage. It is the number that determines whether the plant meets its obligations in the week that matters, which is never the design week.

Why the gaps matter

Genuine headroom is valuable because every additional megalitre a day can defer the point at which new capacity must be built.

The gap between nominal and theoretical is where old plants are underrated and new plants are oversold. The gap between theoretical and demonstrated is where the plant’s real behaviour lives and where most capacity disputes are decided. The gap between demonstrated and constrained is the plant’s remaining headroom, and it is the single most valuable number in a capital plan, because every additional megalitre a day of headroom that can be shown to exist can defer the point at which new capacity must be built. The gap between constrained and resilient is what the utility is actually risking.

A study that reports one number reports none of these gaps. It cannot, because the gaps only appear when the five numbers are determined separately.

Consider two hypothetical plants. The first is nominally rated at a hundred megalitres a day, but its operating record only shows sustained compliant operation to about seventy-eight under its difficult source-water conditions, because the solids handling has never kept up beyond that under high turbidity and operations have quietly learnt not to push it. The second is rated at thirty-five and has run reliably above forty-five for years, with the record to prove it, while the utility plans an upgrade on the assumption that thirty-five is the ceiling. In both cases the number in the planning documents is the nominal one. In both cases the decision is being made on the least informative of the five.

Capacity is an envelope

The reason a single number cannot describe capacity is that a plant does not have a maximum flow. It has a region, in the space of flow and influent condition, within which it can reliably meet its treatment obligations. Outside that region it fails, and it fails for different reasons at different edges.

Picture flow on one axis and influent load on the other: turbidity, colour, algae, ammonia, whatever drives the process. The plant’s operating envelope is the area it can occupy. At the high-flow, low-load edge the binding constraint is usually hydraulic. At the high-load edge it is usually solids, or chemistry, or contact time. At the corner where both are high it may be something else entirely, often the residuals system or the operators’ ability to respond.

Drawing that envelope is the real capacity study. Each edge has a constraint that governs it, and each constraint has an evidence class. Some edges are measured, because the plant has been there and the record shows what happened. Some are calculated. Some are assumed, and the assumption should be marked as such with the evidence that would resolve it. The finished envelope tells the utility how much the plant can do, what it would take to push each edge outward, and which edge is worth pushing.

How we determine it

We start with the operating record, not the design.

The method is not exotic. It is a discipline about what counts as evidence and in what order it is consulted.

We start with the operating record, not the design. The record is the only source of demonstrated capacity, and it contains more than most utilities realise. Flow, water quality, chemical use, sludge production, backwash frequency, energy: in combination, over a few years, they show where the plant has been and what it did there. The first task is to reconstruct the periods of highest stress and establish, unit by unit, what was binding.

We then do the calculations, but as a check on the record rather than a substitute for it. Where the calculated capacity of a unit exceeds what the plant has demonstrated, the interesting question is why. Sometimes the plant has never been asked. Sometimes there is a constraint the calculation does not see.

We look explicitly for the constraints that unit calculations miss: the coupled ones. Recycle streams, residuals handling, chemical storage and delivery, instrumentation range, control loop tuning, staffing, and the plant’s physical ability to be operated at the required intensity. These are where constrained capacity usually lives.

And we separate resilience from routine. What does the envelope look like with the largest unit out of service? With the raw water at its historical worst? Both at once? That is the number the business case should be tested against.

The output is a capacity envelope with the binding constraint named at each edge, the evidence class of each stated, and the assumptions that would change the picture flagged with their severity. That is what we mean by BreakPoint Capacity.

What to ask of any capacity figure

If you are being given a capacity number rather than producing one, three questions will tell you which of the five you are looking at.

Which of the five is this? If the answer is "the design capacity," you have the nominal figure and should treat it as a statement of intent.

What is the binding constraint at that flow, and how do you know? If nobody can name it, the figure is theoretical and the coupled constraints have not been examined.

When did the plant last operate there, and what happened? If the answer is "never," the figure has not been demonstrated, and no amount of calculation makes it so.

A plant can reach a flow and still fail to do its job. The question is never whether it can reach the flow. It is where, across the conditions it will actually face, it can reliably meet its obligations. That is an envelope, and it takes an envelope to describe it.
