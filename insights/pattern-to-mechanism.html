Insights · Data interpretation

From pattern to mechanism.

Dr Brendan Gladman, Partner, Digital and Data, and Peter Hillis, Founder and Principal · September 2026

Water utilities are not short of data. A mid-sized treatment plant generates tens of thousands of readings a day, and behind the SCADA historian sit years of laboratory results, maintenance records, chemical deliveries, energy bills and operator logs. What utilities are short of is the step between the data and a decision, and that step is not a data problem.

Data science exposes patterns. Process science asks whether a pattern has a physically credible mechanism, whether the alternative explanations survive, and what test would tell them apart. Decision Intelligence, as we practise it, is the discipline of doing both in one team, in that order.

A pattern is a question, not an answer

A pattern is a question, not an answer.

Consider a chloraminated distribution system in which the residual at a downstream sample point has been falling faster than expected. An analyst plots residual against every other variable in the historian and finds a strong inverse relationship with nitrite. When nitrite rises, residual falls. The correlation is real, the chart is clean, and the conclusion writes itself: nitrification is consuming the chloramine.

It may be. But the same pattern is consistent with at least three other stories. Nitrite could be a consequence of the low residual rather than its cause, appearing because the residual dropped for some other reason and let the bacteria grow. Both could be driven by a third variable, such as detention time in a reservoir that lengthens in low demand, producing both decay and nitrite in parallel. Or the apparent relationship could arise from the measurement system itself, in which case the residual has not fallen as far as the record says and the plant is chasing an instrument.

Each of these is capable of producing the observed pattern. The chart alone cannot reliably choose between them. The data science has done its job by finding the pattern; what it cannot do is say what the pattern means, because meaning lives in the physics and chemistry of the system, not in the correlation.

Mechanism is what makes it engineering

To choose between the stories you have to ask what each one would predict beyond the chart, and go and look.

If nitrification is the cause, the relationship should be strongest where detention time is longest, nitrite should lead residual in time, and free ammonia should be present upstream to feed it. If nitrite is a consequence, residual should fall first and nitrite follow. If a reservoir is driving both, the pattern should track demand and reservoir level and weaken when the reservoir is bypassed. If the measurement is the mechanism, a grab sample by a different method should disagree with the online reading, and the disagreement should track the same conditions.

Every one of those is a testable prediction, and most can be tested from data the utility already holds. The ones that cannot point to the measurement worth making. That is the transition from pattern to mechanism: the correlation generates hypotheses, the physics ranks them, and the discriminating test is chosen to separate the ones that remain.

This is not a subtle point, but it is routinely skipped, because a strong correlation feels like an answer and the pressure in an operational problem is to act. Acting on a pattern without a mechanism is how utilities end up with chemical dosing changes that make no difference, capital works aimed at the wrong unit, and, most expensively, confidence that a problem has been solved when it has only been correlated.

What the process scientist brings

The reason this needs a process scientist rather than a better algorithm is that mechanism is domain knowledge. Knowing that an online residual measurement can be affected by analyser chemistry, sample conditioning, calibration and matrix effects, that monochloramine decay has an autocatalytic pathway, that a particular reservoir stratifies in summer, that a dosing pump’s turndown limits how low a ratio can actually be controlled: none of that is in the historian, and all of it determines which hypothesis survives.

Domain knowledge also tells you which patterns not to chase. A dataset with enough variables will always contain correlations, and most of them mean nothing. A process scientist looking at the same list of correlations will recognise most of them as artefacts of shared drivers, sampling schedules or instrument behaviour, and will go straight to the two or three that could be causal. That triage is worth more than any amount of additional modelling.

What the data scientist brings

A pattern without a mechanism is not evidence of anything in particular. A mechanism without a pattern is an opinion.

It cuts both ways. Process scientists have their own failure mode, which is to know the mechanism before looking at the data and then find it there. One of us has four decades of process experience, which is a strong prior, and strong priors are exactly what a well-designed data analysis is meant to test.

Good data work protects against that. It insists that the pattern be shown before it is explained, that the explanation be tested against the periods where it should fail, and that the alternative explanations be given the same chance. It also finds things the mechanism-first mind would never look for: the relationship nobody predicted, the regime change in the record that marks when something in the plant was altered, the diurnal cycle in a variable that was assumed constant.

Neither discipline is sufficient. A pattern without a mechanism is not evidence of anything in particular. A mechanism without a pattern is an opinion.

The sequence

This is not a second BreakPoint method. It is what Frame, Evidence, Reason, Challenge, Decide looks like when the evidence happens to be operational data. In practice the work follows a short sequence that we use on every Decision Intelligence engagement.

Data to pattern. Assemble the record, clean it honestly, and find the relationships that are actually there. This is the analyst’s craft and it is not trivial: the same historian export often contains two instruments’ worth of readings under one tag name, and the first pattern found is frequently an artefact of that.

Pattern to hypotheses. For each pattern that could matter, write down every mechanism that would produce it, including the boring ones: instrument, sampling, a change in operation nobody logged.

Hypotheses to test. For each hypothesis, state what else would be true if it were the cause, and check. Most can be checked against data already held. The remainder define the measurement to make next.

Test to decision. With the surviving mechanism established, and its evidence class stated, the decision is usually obvious. Change the dose, fix the analyser, bypass the reservoir, or, often enough, do nothing, because the pattern turned out to be harmless.

The output of that sequence is not a dashboard. It is a short statement of what is physically happening in the plant, how confident we are, what evidence that rests on, and what to do about it.

Why this matters more as the data grows

Data reveals the pattern. Process science tests the mechanism. The decision belongs to whoever has done both.

The volume of operational data is increasing far faster than the number of people who can interpret it physically. That gap is where the risk sits. It is now easy to produce a plausible-looking analysis of a treatment plant with no understanding of the plant at all, and the more polished the analysis, the harder it is to see that the mechanism step has been skipped.

The corrective is not less analysis. It is the discipline of asking, of every pattern that is about to drive a decision, what the mechanism is and how we know. Data reveals the pattern. Process science tests the mechanism. The decision belongs to whoever has done both.
