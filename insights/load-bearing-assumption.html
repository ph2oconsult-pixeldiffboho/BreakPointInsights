Insights · Decision robustness

The load-bearing assumption.

Peter Hillis, Founder and Principal · September 2026

Every design basis contains more assumptions than anyone has time to test. A concept study for a treatment plant upgrade will carry a hundred of them, some explicit, most not: influent loads, peak factors, water quality percentiles, settling characteristics, membrane flux, sludge yields, chemical doses, equipment availability, growth forecasts, discount rates. A reviewer who tries to challenge all of them produces a long list of comments and changes nothing.

The purpose of technical assurance is not to eliminate uncertainty. It is to find the small number of assumptions that are capable of changing the decision, and to establish how well those particular assumptions are known. I call these the load-bearing assumptions, and finding them is the most valuable thing a reviewer can do.

Most assumptions do not matter

The purpose of technical assurance is not to eliminate uncertainty. It is to find the small number of assumptions capable of changing the decision.

This is the point that gets lost. In a well-framed decision, most of the inputs can move across their plausible range without changing the answer. The recommended option stays the recommended option; the capacity stays adequate; the cost stays within the band the board was given. Those assumptions are real, and they should be recorded, but they are not where the risk lives.

A few assumptions behave differently. Move one of them across its plausible range and the ranking of options changes, or the plant that was adequate is not, or the cost moves out of its band. These are the load-bearing ones. The decision rests on them in the way a floor rests on a beam, and the question that matters is whether the beam has been inspected.

Load-bearing status is not a property of the assumption. It is a property of the assumption in combination with the decision.

The trouble is that load-bearing status is not visible from the assumption itself. It is a property of the assumption in combination with the decision. The same peak factor might be trivial in a plant with hydraulic headroom and decisive in one without. You cannot find the load-bearing assumptions by reading the assumptions list. You find them by asking, for each one, what happens to the decision if it is wrong.

Where they hide

Some patterns recur often enough to be worth naming.

The assumption that fixes the constraint. In many treatment decisions one unit or one parameter becomes binding, and the assumptions that establish that constraint are the first place I look. If the clarifier is the constraint, the assumed settling velocity or solids flux is the assumption the whole capacity number rests on. If the disinfection contact tank is the constraint, it is the assumed baffling factor. Find the binding constraint and you have usually found the first load-bearing assumption.

The assumption inherited from a different plant. Design guide values and previous projects supply a great many inputs, and most transfer well enough. The ones that do not are the ones tied to the specific water or the specific process: an assumed coagulant dose from a plant with different natural organic matter, a sludge volume index from a plant with a different sludge age, a membrane flux from a plant with a different pretreatment. If the number came from somewhere else, ask what would have to be true here for it to transfer, and whether anyone has checked.

The assumption that has never been tested because the plant has never been there. A capacity assessment often extrapolates beyond the highest flow or worst water quality the plant has actually seen. Everything about the plant’s behaviour beyond that point is assumed, and the assumption is load-bearing precisely because the upgrade is intended to take the plant there.

The assumption that appears to be conservative. I wrote about this in the first of these pieces. A conservative assumption for one failure mode is frequently an aggressive one for another, and the direction is rarely stated. An assumed high influent load is safe for tank sizing and unsafe for the energy and chemical budget the business case rests on.

The assumption that is actually a decision. Some inputs that look technical are policy choices in disguise. A design horizon, a level of service, a percentile of water quality to design for: these are decisions the owner should make consciously, and when they arrive in a design basis as numbers it is usually because someone had to pick one to get on with the work. If the option ranking depends on them, they need to be put back in front of the person entitled to decide.

How to find them

The method is a structured sensitivity test, but done on the decision rather than on the model.

Start by stating the decision precisely. Not "assess the upgrade" but "confirm that Option B, at the stated cost, will meet the stated obligations to the stated horizon." The sharper the decision, the easier it is to see what could overturn it.

List the assumptions, including the ones that are not written down. The unwritten ones are usually the most dangerous: the assumption that the raw water will not deteriorate, that the operators will run the plant as designed, that the vendor’s performance claim holds at full scale.

For each assumption, ask what plausible range it could take and what happens to the decision at each end of that range. Plausible is the operative word. It is not useful to ask what happens if the influent load doubles; it is useful to ask what happens at the ninety-fifth percentile of the record, or at the load the catchment forecast implies in fifteen years.

The assumptions that change the decision somewhere in their plausible range are the load-bearing ones. In my experience there are surprisingly few, often fewer than five.

For those, and only those, establish the evidence class. Measured, calculated, literature-supported, modelled, inferred, assumed, heuristic. A load-bearing assumption that is measured on this plant is a small risk. A load-bearing assumption that is inferred from another plant, or simply assumed, is where the decision is exposed, and it is where the next piece of evidence should be bought.

What to do with them

Once the load-bearing assumptions are named and classified, three things become possible that were not possible before.

The reviewer can say, in one page, what the decision depends on. That page is worth more to a board than the hundred-page report behind it.

The project can spend its remaining investigation budget where it changes the decision. A settling test, a pilot trial, a month of enhanced monitoring, a site visit to the plant the flux figure came from: each of these is cheap relative to the capital it protects, and each is only worth doing if the assumption it tests is load-bearing.

And the decision can be made with its conditions attached. "Proceed with Option B, provided the settling characteristics assumed in the design are confirmed by testing before detailed design" is a better decision than "proceed with Option B", because it keeps the beam under inspection.

A closing thought on humility

The remedy is not to trust judgement less. It is to make judgement show its working.

The reason this discipline matters is that experienced engineers are good at being confident and not always good at knowing which of their confident statements is doing the work. I include myself. After four decades I have a well-developed sense of what is likely to matter, and it is usually right, and the occasions when it has been wrong have all had the same shape: an assumption I regarded as background turned out to be structural.

The remedy is not to trust judgement less. It is to make judgement show its working, so that the load-bearing assumptions are on the page where someone else can look at them.
