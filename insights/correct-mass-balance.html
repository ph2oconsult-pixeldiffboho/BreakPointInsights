When a correct mass balance gives the wrong engineering answer.

Dr Brendan Gladman, Partner, Digital and Data, and Peter Hillis, Founder and Principal · September 2026

A mass balance is the most trusted calculation in process engineering, and rightly so. Mass is conserved. If the inputs are right and the arithmetic closes, the answer is right. That is exactly why a mass balance can be dangerous: its correctness is so easy to check that people stop asking whether it is answering the right question.

Between us we have seen more expensive mistakes come out of correct mass balances than out of wrong ones. A wrong mass balance gets caught. A correct one gets built.

The balance closes. The plant does not.

A wrong mass balance gets caught. A correct one gets built.

Here is the shape of the problem. A design team calculates the solids produced by a water treatment plant under a design condition, sizes the sludge handling to match, and checks the balance. Solids in equals solids out. The calculation is reviewed and approved.

The plant is built and, under the design condition, it works. Then the river floods. Raw water turbidity goes from the design value to ten times the design value for a week. The clarifiers produce solids at a rate the thickener was never sized for. The thickener fills, the clarifier desludging is throttled to protect it, the sludge blanket rises, the filters take the carry-over, backwash frequency doubles, the backwash water recovery system fills, and the plant is now recycling its own problem. Output drops at precisely the moment demand is highest.

Nothing in the mass balance was wrong. The design condition was a single point, and the plant lives in a distribution. A balance that closes at the median says little about performance in the tail, yet those infrequent high-load conditions can govern residuals storage, processing capacity and the plant’s ability to maintain production.

Five ways a correct balance misleads

It is a steady-state snapshot and the plant is not. Most design mass balances close over an hour or a day by averaging away the accumulation and depletion that happen between units. Real plants are driven by diurnal flow, storm events, seasonal water quality, batch processes like backwashing and desludging, and the operators’ responses to all of them. A thickener that balances on a daily average can still overflow at three in the afternoon. The question is not whether the mass balances over the day but whether the storage between the units can carry the difference between the peak and the average.

It is a point and the plant is a distribution. Every input to a mass balance is a chosen number, and the choice is where the judgement lives. Design at the median and the residuals system is undersized. Design at the maximum and the plant is expensive and probably still fails, because the maximum in the record is not the maximum that will occur. The useful approach is to run the balance across the distribution, decide which percentile the plant must survive, and make that a stated decision rather than an implied one.

The balance is correct for the species you counted. A chloramination system balances chlorine and ammonia by mass and arrives at a ratio. The ratio is correct. But chlorine and ammonia react to form more than one species, and which species dominates depends on the local ratio at the point of mixing, not the bulk ratio downstream. A dose can produce the intended bulk chlorine-to-ammonia ratio at the sample point while creating local conditions at the injection point, in concentration, pH and order of addition, that favour unwanted chloramine species before mixing is complete. The mass balance sees the totals; the chemistry happens in the detail the totals conceal.

The balance is correct and the hydraulics will not deliver it. A biological reactor is designed with a mass balance on oxygen, and the aeration system is sized to supply it. The balance is correct for the reactor as a whole. But if the reactor is short-circuiting, or the inlet momentum is driving flow past the aeration grid, part of the volume is doing most of the work and part is doing very little. The oxygen is supplied and the balance closes; the treatment is not achieved because the mass was delivered to the wrong place. A lumped mass balance has no spatial dimension. Plants do.

The balance is correct and operationally impossible. A biosolids strategy balances the solids from a wastewater plant against a drying and thermal process and shows the numbers work. They do work, on paper. But the drying step needs a feed at a solids content the dewatering cannot reliably reach, the thermal unit needs a turndown the vendor has never demonstrated, and the whole train needs to be operated by a shift roster that does not exist. The mass balance says the process is feasible. Feasibility was never the question. The question was whether it could be operated.

What the balance is for

A correct mass balance tells you that a design is not impossible. It does not tell you that the design will work.

None of this is an argument against mass balances. It is an argument for knowing what they are: a necessary check that says nothing about sufficiency.

A correct mass balance tells you that a design is not impossible. It does not tell you that the design will work. Working depends on dynamics, distributions, chemistry, hydraulics and operability, and each of those needs its own evidence.

In practice we treat a mass balance as the frame for a set of questions rather than as the answer:

At what percentile of the input distribution was this balance done, and who decided that was the right one?

Where is the storage between units, and can it absorb the difference between peak and average at each step?

For the reactions that matter, is the ratio that governs the chemistry the same as the ratio in the balance, and if not, where do they differ?

Does the hydraulic behaviour of each unit deliver the mass to where the balance assumes it goes?

Can the plant as balanced actually be run, by the people who will run it, with the equipment that will be installed?

If those questions have evidence-based answers, the balance is doing its job. If they do not, the balance is correct and the plant is still a guess.

The tell

If those questions have evidence-based answers, the balance is doing its job. If they do not, the balance is correct and the plant is still a guess.

There is a reliable sign that a mass balance is being asked to carry more than it can. It is the phrase "the numbers work."

When we hear it in a review, we ask which numbers, under what conditions, and what would have to be true about the plant for the numbers to describe it. The answers are usually available and usually reassuring. When they are not, the phrase was covering for an absence of evidence, and the mass balance, correct as it was, had become the assumption the whole decision rested on.
